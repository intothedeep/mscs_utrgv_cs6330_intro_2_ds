\textbf{How to read the charts.} Each kind of panel answers one question.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.2\linewidth}p{0.72\linewidth}}
  \toprule
  Chart & Question it answers \\
  \midrule
  Histogram / PDF & What is the shape: where is the peak, how long is the tail? \\
  \midrule
  PMF (airports) & How many airports have exactly $k$ routes? (Route counts are whole numbers.) \\
  \midrule
  CDF & What share lies at or below $x$? Where is the largest gap $D$ between data and model? \\
  \midrule
  CCDF & What share lies at or above $x$? On log-log axes a power law is a straight line. \\
  \midrule
  QQ plot & Does each quantile of the model match the data's? Points on $y = x$ mean yes. \\
  \midrule
  Box plot (movies) & Are the centre, spread and range the same? Are there impossible ratings
    outside 1 to 10? \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Linear axes show where most values are; log-log axes spread out values that span several
orders of magnitude.

\prompt{\textbf{Problem 1.}}

\medskip
\prompt{\textbf{Airport data:}}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \prompt{What is the $\alpha$ if the distribution is power law?}
  \answer{$\alpha = 1.612$, with $x_{\min} = 1$ route.
    Maximum-likelihood estimate $\hat\alpha = 1 + n / \sum_i \ln(x_i / x_{\min})$,
    $x_{\min} = \min_i x_i$, $n = 3409$.
    \begin{align*}
      &n = 3409 && \text{number of airports}\\
      &x_{\min} = \min_i x_i = 1 && \text{smallest route count}\\
      &S = \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{3409} \ln(x_i / 1) = 5569.43 && \text{sum of log ratios}\\
      &n / S = 3409 / 5569.43 = 0.6121 && \\
      &\hat\alpha = 1 + 0.6121 = 1.612 && \text{estimate}
    \end{align*}}

  \item \prompt{What is the $\lambda$ if the distribution is exponential?}
  \answer{$\lambda = 0.0504$. MLE $\hat\lambda = 1/\bar x = 1/19.845$.
    \begin{align*}
      &\textstyle\sum_i x_i = 67652 && \text{total routes}\\
      &\bar x = 67652 / 3409 = 19.845 && \text{mean}\\
      &\hat\lambda = 1 / 19.845 = 0.0504 && \text{estimate}
    \end{align*}}

  \item \prompt{What is $[a, b]$ if the distribution is uniform?}
  \answer{$[a, b] = [1, 915]$, the minimum and maximum route counts (MLE).
    \begin{align*}
      &\hat a = \min_i x_i = 1 && \text{fewest routes}\\
      &\hat b = \max_i x_i = 915 && \text{most routes}
    \end{align*}}

  \item \prompt{What is $\mu$ and $\sigma$ if the distribution is normal?}
  \answer{$\mu = 19.845$, $\sigma = 53.506$ (sample mean and sample standard deviation
    with $n-1$).
    \begin{align*}
      &\hat\mu = \bar x = 19.845 && \text{mean, as in the exponential fit}\\
      &\textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 = 9{,}756{,}906.22 && \text{sum of squared deviations}\\
      &9{,}756{,}906.22 / (3409 - 1) = 2862.94 && \text{sample variance}\\
      &\hat\sigma = \sqrt{2862.94} = 53.506 && \text{standard deviation}
    \end{align*}}

  % Start the chart part of the airport answers on a fresh page.
  \clearpage
  \prompt{(These can be CDF, PDF, or QQplot)}

  \textbf{Note on the axes.} In every chart on linear axes, the routes-per-airport axis is
  cut at 200 on purpose: 98\% of airports have at most 200 routes, and on a full 0--915 axis
  they would be squeezed into the left edge. The whole distribution, including the tail up
  to 915 routes and the model samples beyond it, is shown in the log-log chart next to each
  linear one. The share of the data and of each model sample beyond 200 is printed on the
  linear panels.

  For (e) to (i):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Samples.} $n = 3409$ random values drawn from each fitted model with
          numpy's random generator (seed 20701313; one generator for all eight samples, so every
          figure can be rebuilt exactly).
          \begin{itemize}
            \item Power law: numpy has no power-law sampler, so I used inverse transform
                  sampling. Draw $u$ uniformly from $[0, 1)$ and set
                  $x = x_{\min}(1-u)^{-1/(\alpha-1)}$, which solves $u = F(x)$ for the fitted
                  CDF $F(x) = 1 - (x/x_{\min})^{-(\alpha-1)}$.
            \item Exponential: \texttt{rng.exponential($1/\lambda$)} (numpy takes the mean
                  $1/\lambda$, not $\lambda$).
            \item Uniform: \texttt{rng.uniform($a$, $b$)}. Normal: \texttt{rng.normal($\mu$, $\sigma$)}.
          \end{itemize}
          The samples are real numbers, not whole route counts: they contain values such as
          1.08, and the normal sample contains negative values.
    \item \textbf{Colours.} Data in blue, model sample in orange.
    \item \textbf{Layout of the airport figures.} Rows PDF, CDF, CCDF and QQ, with linear axes
          on the left and log-log axes on the right; the data's median and mean
          are marked in every distribution panel, and each CDF/CCDF shows its value at $x = 1$.
    \item \textbf{$D$.} Two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance: the largest vertical gap
          between the two CDFs (0 = identical).
  \end{itemize}

  \item \prompt{Screen shot of the visualization of distribution of data}
  \answerfig{p1a_0_data}{Airport routes: empirical distribution ($n = 3409$). Rows: PDF, PMF, CDF, CCDF. Left column on linear axes, right column on log-log axes.}

  \item \prompt{Screen shot of the visualization of distribution of data if it was power law}
  \answerfig{p1a_1_powerlaw}{Airport routes vs.\ power law ($\alpha = 1.612$, $x_{\min} = 1$). $D = 0.209$.}

  \item \prompt{Screen shot of the visualization of distribution of data if it was exponential}
  \answerfig{p1a_2_exponential}{Airport routes vs.\ exponential ($\lambda = 0.0504$). $D = 0.397$.}

  \item \prompt{Screen shot of the visualization of distribution of data if it was uniform}
  \answerfig{p1a_3_uniform}{Airport routes vs.\ uniform on $[1, 915]$. $D = 0.856$.}

  \item \prompt{Screen shot of the visualization of distribution of data if it was normal}
  \answerfig{p1a_4_normal}{Airport routes vs.\ normal ($\mu = 19.8$, $\sigma = 53.5$). $D = 0.365$.}

  \item \prompt{What is the distribution of data based on your observation? Discuss}
  \answer{%
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
      \toprule
      & power law & exponential & uniform & normal \\
      \midrule
      KS distance $D$ (data vs model sample) & \textbf{0.209} & 0.397 & 0.856 & 0.365 \\
      \midrule
      where the gap is largest, $x$ & 1 & 6 & 60 & 0.98 \\
      \midrule
      data CDF $F_{\text{data}}(x)$ there & 0.209 & 0.648 & 0.922 & 0.000 \\
      \midrule
      model-sample CDF $F_{\text{model}}(x)$ there & 0.000 & 0.251 & 0.065 & 0.365 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

    \textbf{How $D$ is computed.} $D$ is the two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance from
    \texttt{scipy.stats.ks\_2samp}, computed on the raw values: the largest vertical gap
    between the data's CDF and the model sample's CDF (0 = identical, 1 = no overlap),
    \[
      D = \max_x \bigl|F_{\text{data}}(x) - F_{\text{model}}(x)\bigr| .
    \]

    \textbf{Conclusion: the airport data follows a power law.}

    \textbf{Why the power law fits} (Figure~\ref{fig:p1a_1_powerlaw}):
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Lowest $D$:} 0.209, against 0.365 to 0.856 for the other three models.
      \item \textbf{PDF shape:} the data and the model sample match on both kinds of axes.
            On linear axes the bars have almost the same heights; on log-log axes both follow
            the same straight line up to about 300 routes.
      \item \textbf{QQ plot (log-log):} the points lie on $y = x$ up to about 60 routes.
      \item \textbf{Skewness:} the data is strongly right-skewed (6.0), and only the power
            law is that skewed. Over the data's range (up to 915 routes) its sample has 6.8;
            the exponential's has 2.0, the uniform's and the normal's 0. (With its giant
            sampled airports included the power-law sample reaches 47.5: for
            $\hat\alpha < 4$ the model's skewness is infinite.)
      \item \textbf{80/20:} the top 20\% of airports hold 83.8\% of all routes, close to the
            80/20 rule that power laws are known for. The power-law sample gives 87.1\% over
            the data's range; the exponential gives 51.9\%, the normal 41.9\% (positive
            values only) and the uniform 35.6\%. This is the same fact as the skewness seen
            from another side: a few hubs with hundreds of routes carry most of the total,
            which is also why the mean (19.8) is five times the median (4).
    \end{itemize}

    \textbf{Why the others fail:} their QQ plot shape is different; the points leave
    $y = x$ almost from the start.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Exponential} (Figure~\ref{fig:p1a_2_exponential}, $D = 0.397$): too
            steep, below the line for small airports and above it for hubs.
      \item \textbf{Uniform} (Figure~\ref{fig:p1a_3_uniform}, $D = 0.856$): every point far
            below the line.
      \item \textbf{Normal} (Figure~\ref{fig:p1a_4_normal}, $D = 0.365$): too steep, with
            negative model quantiles that no airport has.
    \end{itemize}}
\end{enumerate}

      &\hat\alpha = 1 + 0.8505 = 1.851 && \text{estimate}

    \begin{align*}
      &\textstyle\sum_i x_i = 27348.7 && \text{sum of votes}\\
      &\bar x = 27348.7 / 4392 = 6.227 && \text{mean}\\

\prompt{\textbf{Problem 2.}}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \prompt{Screenshot of distribution/histogram of the age}
  \answer{\textbf{How the ages were computed.}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Date format:} \texttt{dd-Mon-yy}. 277 rows use a 4-digit year instead:
            129 are the placeholder \texttt{0000} (dropped, see below) and 148 are real
            years from 1732 to 1899, which are kept as written. All 148 give ages of 127 or
            more, so the age filter removes them later.
      \item \textbf{Century:} every 2-digit year is read as 19\textit{yy}. IMDB-WIKI was
            collected in 2015, and the counts per year fall smoothly down to \texttt{00}
            with no jump into the 2000s.
      \item \textbf{Missing dates:} 129 rows have year \texttt{0000} (the year is unknown),
            so no age can be computed and they are dropped.
      \item \textbf{Age:} 2026 minus birth year, month and day ignored (professor's
            instruction, 2026-09-23).
      \item \textbf{Filter:} keep $1 \le \text{age} \le 99$, as the assignment asks. 7{,}500
            rows are over 99 and dropped; none are under 1.
      \item \textbf{Result:} \textbf{453{,}089} people; mean 56.79, median 55, range 27 to
            99. The minimum is 27 because the latest birth year is 1999
            ($2026 - 1999 = 27$).
    \end{itemize}}
  \answerfig{p2_0_age_all}{All ages in 2026 before the $1 \le \text{age} \le 99$ filter
    ($n = 460{,}589$). Blue bars are kept, coral bars are removed; log $y$ axis. Median 55 (green dotted), mean 57.65 (purple dash-dot).}
  \answerfig{p2_1_age}{Age in 2026 ($n = 453{,}089$). Median 55 (green dotted), mean 56.79 (purple dash-dot).}

  \item \prompt{Screenshot of distribution/histogram of the first digit of age}
  \answerfig{p2_2_first_digit}{First digit of age, with the uniform level $1/9$ (grey dashed) and a normal fitted to the digits (green dashed, $\mu = 5.22$, $\sigma = 1.43$). Median 5 (green dotted), mean 5.22 (purple dash-dot), mode 5 (gold); the shaded band is mean $\pm$ $\sigma$ ($\sigma = 1.43$, variance 2.05).}

  \item \prompt{Screenshot of distribution/histogram of the last digit of age}
  \answerfig{p2_3_last_digit}{Last digit of age, with the uniform level $1/10$ (dashed). Median 5 (green dotted), mean 4.55 (purple dash-dot), mode 7 (gold); the shaded band is mean $\pm$ $\sigma$ ($\sigma = 2.87$, variance 8.24).}

  \item \prompt{Discussion (what are those distribution?) Why do you think it is like this?}
  \answer{%
    \textbf{The first digit is approximately normal; the last digit is approximately uniform.}

    \textbf{First digit: approximately normal}, because:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
      \item It is bell-shaped, with one peak at 5 that falls off on both sides.
      \item The mean (5.22), median (5) and mode (5) are all in the middle and almost equal.
      \item A normal fitted to the digits ($\mu = 5.22$, $\sigma = 1.43$) follows the bars
            within 5.3 points (Figure~\ref{fig:p2_2_first_digit}).
      \item For ages 30 to 99 the first digit is the decade of age, and age itself has one
            hump (Figure~\ref{fig:p2_1_age}).
      \item It is not exactly normal: it is slightly right-skewed ($+0.53$), digit 2 is small
            because ages are measured in 2026, so the youngest actor (born 1999) is already 27,
            and digit 1 never appears because no age is between 10 and 19.
    \end{enumerate}

    \textbf{Last digit: approximately uniform}, because:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
      \item The bars are flat, with every digit within $10\% \pm 1.2$ points
            (Figure~\ref{fig:p2_3_last_digit}).
      \item The mean (4.55) and variance (8.24) match a uniform on 0--9 (4.5 and
            $(10^2 - 1)/12 = 8.25$).
      \item The last digit is the age modulo 10, so every block of ten ages contains each digit
            once, and age counts change slowly within a block.
      \item The small gaps are year-to-year noise in births (Figure~\ref{fig:p2_1_age}), not a
            preference for any digit.
    \end{enumerate}}
\end{itemize}
